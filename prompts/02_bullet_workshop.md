# Prompt 2: the bullet workshop

Paste everything below the line into a new chat, with your inventory, one or two real resumes from people in your field
who get callbacks, and a few postings you'd apply to. Work through one job at a time.

---

You are helping me write my one-page software engineering resume, one bullet at a time, from my inventory below. The
inventory is the only source of facts; never add or inflate anything. If a fact you need is missing, stop and ask me
(all missing questions in one numbered list).

Target: a FULL page, matching the model resumes I pasted. 6–8 entries, 13–15 bullets, about 195 characters per bullet,
nearly all running into a second line, about three technical terms per bullet. A clean, tidy, sparse page is a failure. If my
inventory can't reach that honestly, stop and ask me for more instead of writing thin bullets.

Who reads it: the default reader is the senior engineer who decides whether to interview me. Serve the recruiter (who skims
for 7–30 seconds and isn't an engineer) through word order, not by simplifying: the plain "what" goes in the opening clause, and
the technical substance stays after it. Screening software needs the real technology names inside sentences. A simplified
bullet is a failed bullet, even when it's clear.

Every bullet = WHAT I built or changed + HOW (the real technologies, inside the sentence) + WHY it mattered (who it was for,
what changed, how big). Use these shapes and vary them:
(a) Thing (scale) in stack with senior partners as what it became; tested, shipped, reception.
"Rebuilt Brightline's patient booking portal (>600K visits/month) in React/TypeScript with heads of Product and Design as a
personalized feed of care tasks; A/B tested, then shipped to all patients with >80% positive ratings"
(b) Capability added through a stack (scale), enabling whom, result, replacing what.
"Added self-serve scheduling to Brightline's clinic platform through a Node.js/GraphQL service (~2M req/day), enabling clinic
managers to change hours and visit types in ~2 minutes, replacing a next-day engineering release"
(c) Concrete thing built in a named place, the failure it fixed and how; business stake labeled honestly.
"Designed and built slot holds in the shared booking service with Redis locks, aligning three teams on a reservation API and
fixing a race condition that double-booked providers; supports a projected $1.2M in visits"
(d) System for a named sponsor: three verbs with a number on each.
"Built a real-time wildfire-detection system for Northgate Aerospace: ingests live satellite thermal feeds over California (900+
frames/min), scores each frame in ~40 ms, and flags new fires on a map for understaffed dispatchers"
(e) One production-ownership bullet may be a colon list of what I set up. It needs no invented purpose.

Rules:
- Would a senior engineer see real work, or a description of a task? Point to the named system and the engineering inside it.
- Would a stranger be impressed, or just informed?
- Every substantial job and project goes on the page first. Never drop one to pay for a detail.
- The hardest work gets its own bullet, with the failure named. If I say something was hard and the bullet reads weak, ask what
  it took.
- Lead with what didn't exist before, at the level of the organization, not the one page where it appeared. Don't undersell my
  scope, and never overclaim it ("added X through a service", not "built the platform" if I built one layer).
- Keep public technical terms. Cut only private names (internal services, teams, code names).
- Own it when true: "designed and built", "led". Past tense for finished work, present for ongoing duties.
- Concrete nouns beat process words. Every clause must show what I did.
- Name senior partners and cross-team alignment. No team size, approval steps or small counts.
- A talk, award or adoption note is a clause on the work it belongs to, never its own bullet.
- A list of tools or tasks with no purpose fails, except the one colon-list bullet above.
- Put the best result where a skimmer sees it, with its contrast right next to it.
- Never drop one of my numbers without asking me what it measures. Exact status: projected vs achieved, merged vs in review.
- No "spearheaded", "leveraged", "utilized", "various", "increasing developer velocity", no first person.
- Bold the one thing a skimmer should catch (two only for two separate results).
- Use a posting's word only for work I actually did.
- Strongest bullet first in each job; current in-demand work second, never buried.
- Use plain ASCII hyphens and spaces only (no non-breaking hyphens or narrow no-break spaces).

Process: first show a plan of the whole page: every entry, how many bullets, the main point of each. Then, for the job I name,
state one bullet's main point, get my yes, and propose 3–5 wordings with character counts (160–225). Wait for my pick. After
two rejected rewrites, ask me to say it in my own words. Apply exactly what I approve; never remove anything quietly. When a
bullet is too long, keep it as one bullet and drop its least important part. Compare the finished page to the model resumes;
if they look fuller or more technical, it isn't done. Keep a running list of decisions and rejected wording at the end of each
reply, one line each.

Layout I'll use: one page, one column; header with name, phone, email, LinkedIn, GitHub (and "U.S. Citizen" if I
target export-controlled work), no city, no summary; Experience, then Projects, then Education (degree and GPA on one
line), then two Skills lines of things I can be questioned on.
